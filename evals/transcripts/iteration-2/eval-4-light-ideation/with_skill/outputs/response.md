Running this fast — a few minutes, single pass, no research. It's a fixable operational thing, not a "what should this company become" question, so it doesn't earn the heavy version.

**Sharpened brief:** Keep a whole company holding a compatible, current picture of where things are going and what everyone else is doing — for people who suspect the ritual is theater, when you can't compel anyone to turn up, and when both budget and the exec team's prep time are shrinking.

**The obvious answer (baseline):** Make it shorter and better produced, add live Q&A, record it for the people who miss it, celebrate wins and shout people out, survey everyone on what they'd want. Possibly mandate it.

Everything below is measured against that.

---

### Presence confers power

Give the session one genuinely scarce thing to allocate in public — a slot on next quarter's roadmap, a headcount req, a week of platform-team time, a budget line. The people in the room decide it; the people not in the room don't. Attendance stops being a civic duty and becomes the price of a vote.

- Why it's not the baseline: every baseline move tries to make the session more *worth watching*. This makes it worth *being at*, which is a different variable.
- What has to be true: leadership is willing to actually be bound by it. A fake vote is worse than no vote — it's the single most corrosive thing you can do to an already-skeptical room.
- Failure mode: it gets captured by whoever is loudest, and quiet teams lose resources they'd have won on the merits. Start with something small and genuinely marginal.

### Thirty small jobs instead of one big audience

Break the session into 20–30 tiny named roles — timekeeper, someone who reads the customer complaint of the month out loud, someone who introduces the new joiners, someone who runs the five-minute demo, someone who reads out the metric that got worse. Rotate them. Most of them take two minutes of prep.

- Why it's not the baseline: it treats attendance as a network property rather than a content property. Thirty people who can't skip each drag their immediate orbit along, and the room fills from the edges rather than from the stage.
- What has to be true: you have someone willing to run the rota. This is a real recurring job, roughly a few hours a month, and it dies the moment nobody owns it.
- Failure mode: performative busywork if the roles are decorative. Each role has to carry actual content, not ceremony.

### Couple it to the internal job market

Make the session the first place internal openings, team moves, and next-quarter staffing requests get announced — before the internal listings go up, before managers get the email. Nothing else on the agenda has to change.

- Why it's not the baseline: it hitches attendance to personal self-interest rather than to interest in the company's strategy. Self-interest is far more durable and doesn't decay when the news is boring.
- What has to be true: you have enough internal movement for this to be non-empty most months. Below maybe 100 people it probably isn't.
- Failure mode: it makes the session feel like a recruiting channel, and managers may resent their people being poached in public. Some will quietly stop encouraging attendance — which is exactly the signal you'd want to see.

### Only bad news and unsettled things

Ban wins, up-and-to-the-right charts, and recognition from the live slot — those go in writing, where they're honestly better anyway. The live time is for what's going badly, what's genuinely undecided, and one rotating named person whose standing job is to challenge the exec team on the record.

- Why it's not the baseline: the baseline adds celebration to make the session feel better. Celebration is the most summarizable content there is, which is precisely why it doesn't hold anyone. Bad news is scarce, non-summarizable, and it's the thing a skeptical room shows up to check on.
- What has to be true: leadership can tolerate being visibly wrong in front of everyone, repeatedly, without punishing the challenger.
- Failure mode: relentless negativity, or the challenger role becoming a career-limiting move that nobody will take twice. Rotate it and have a senior person take the first turn.

### Teams present upward, not the reverse

Two or three teams present to leadership each session, and leadership responds live and on the spot. The direction of accountability in the room flips.

- Why it's not the baseline: people attend their peers' moments. It also makes the exec team the ones who have to think on their feet, which is the part that can't be pre-written.
- What has to be true: leadership responds substantively rather than saying "great work, thanks." One vague response and the format is dead.
- Failure mode: it becomes a performance review with an audience, and teams start sandbagging what they show. Prior art: this exists at plenty of companies as an engineering or design review — you can copy a working version rather than invent one.

### Cannibalize it on purpose

Instead of defending the live session, build the written version to actually win — the delta since last time, decisions and what was rejected, in a form people genuinely read. Then let the live thing shrink to whatever remains load-bearing, which may be 25 people rather than the whole company.

- Why it's not the baseline: it accepts that half the company not attending might be correct behavior rather than a problem. The company-wide-synchronous format was a choice, and it's the first thing that stops scaling.
- What has to be true: you're willing to define a quorum — the specific roles whose presence makes the session productive — and let everyone else be genuinely optional without penalty.
- Failure mode: you lose the "we're one company" function, which is real and which writing does badly. If that's what the session was mostly for, this trades away the thing you were trying to protect.

### Delta-only briefing with a readback close

Two borrowed mechanisms. Emergency incident command runs on a written plan distributed ahead of the shift, with the live briefing covering only what changed since the last one. Air traffic control requires the receiving party to read a clearance back, so the transfer is verified rather than assumed. Combined: written plan goes out 48 hours ahead, live slot covers only deltas and open questions, and it closes with three named people restating in their own words what they now think the top priority is.

- Why it's not the baseline: the readback is the unusual half. It turns the session from broadcast into a measurement — the mismatches you hear are the actual output, and they're information you currently have no way to get.
- What has to be true: someone writes the plan properly. If the written artifact is thin, the delta briefing is empty and the whole thing collapses in two months.
- Failure mode: readback feels like being quizzed and people dread it. Caveat: I'm working from general knowledge of both practices and couldn't re-verify the specifics this session — worth a ten-minute check before you build on the analogy.

### Cut the decline, don't average it

Before any of the above: "half the company" is almost certainly not a uniform 50%. It's probably near-total in some teams and near-zero in others. Cut the historical attendance by team, manager, tenure, timezone and hire cohort, and check whether the slot started colliding with something — a sprint ceremony, a new office's evening, a school run, a shift pattern.

- Why it's not the baseline: the baseline surveys people about what they'd *want*, which measures stated preference. This measures who actually left and when, which usually names the cause outright — and it frequently turns out to be mechanical rather than motivational.
- What has to be true: you have the attendance data by person over time. If you don't, that's a one-line fix worth making this month.
- Failure mode: none really, beyond the cost. The risk is stopping here — a diagnosis that names a manager or a timezone is easy to file and never act on.

**Cut, and why:**
- *Stop recording it, so live is the only way to get the content.* Effective, and the entire cost lands on the people least able to attend — parents, other timezones, shift workers. You'd buy attendance by taxing exactly the wrong people.
- *Mandate or track it.* Produces bodies, not attention, and destroys the one clean signal you'd use to diagnose the problem.
- *Invite customers, investors or advisors into the room.* Cheap and it does change behavior, but candor drops — and candor is probably already the scarce good here.
- *Auto-generate the update from your existing tools.* Removes the prep tax, but nobody reads auto-generated summaries either. You'd move the problem, not solve it.
- *Personalized per-person briefs.* Highest relevance density of anything I generated, but it needs someone to maintain a live dependency map forever. The cost lands on one person permanently.

**Set diversity:** scored 10 candidate mechanisms — 9.88 effectively distinct lexically, no near-duplicate pairs. Reading them for mechanism rather than wording, I merged two pairs, leaving the 8 above. Two things worth reporting rather than hiding:

- Four independent passes landed on the same place: *the broadcast goes in writing, the live slot is only for what's contested.* When separated generators converge like that, it's usually the real attractor of the problem, not a coincidence. If you only do one thing, do that one.
- Every lens produced a *redesign*. None produced a *diagnosis* — the last idea only appeared when I forced generation outside the existing clusters. Given that, I'd genuinely do the cohort cut first and pick from the other seven second.

**What would change my ranking:**
- Whether you're mostly co-located or mostly distributed. If you're in one or two offices, the social-periphery argument dominates and "presence confers power" and "thirty small jobs" get much stronger. If you're spread across timezones, "cannibalize on purpose" is probably just correct and the rest is rearranging deck chairs. I assumed at least partly distributed.
- Whether the decline is gradual or has a step change in it. A step change means something specific happened — a reorg, a slot move, a layoff, one badly-received session — and the fix is that thing, not the format.
- Who'd actually run any of this. Most of these need a standing owner with a few hours a month: chief of staff, internal comms, or an EA with real authority. If there's no such person, the only ones that survive without one are the cohort cut and cannibalizing it on purpose. Everything else quietly reverts within a quarter.
