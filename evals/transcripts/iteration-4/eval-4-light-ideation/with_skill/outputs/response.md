I'm reading "half showing up" as live joins. If a good chunk of the other half watches the recording, this is a measurement problem rather than an attendance problem and most of what follows is unnecessary.

**Work out which decline this is first.** Four plausible causes: you outgrew the format (nothing is relevant to everyone anymore); substitution (the recap already covers it); calendar pressure (it's the only droppable hour in a full week); or it's consequence-free (nothing said there changes anyone's week). Cheapest test — cross-tab live attendance by team, tenure and timezone against recording views, then skip one month entirely and see what people rebuild on their own. A uniform slow slide points at cost or consequence; a slide concentrated in recent hires points at outgrowing the format.

### Convene on events, not on the calendar
Drop the fixed slot. Meet within 48 hours of something actually happening — a big win, a lost deal, a strategy change — for twenty minutes, no deck. A monthly cadence guarantees most instances have nothing urgent to carry, and an hour that gets filled regardless is what teaches people the hour is optional.
- What has to be true: leadership has real news more often than never and less often than constantly.
- Failure mode: quiet quarters go silent and the habit dies; you lose the rhythm that made it feel like an institution.
- Who runs it: the CEO has to be willing to call it, and someone owns the trigger criteria so it isn't at whim.

### Hand the hour to a different team each month
The hosting team owns the content; execs attend as guests who answer questions rather than present. Obligation to peers pulls harder than obligation upward, and a hosting team brings its own people plus everyone curious about them.
- What has to be true: you have eight-plus teams with something worth an audience, and leadership can tolerate not controlling the narrative.
- Failure mode: quality swings hard, and a bad month costs you more than a mediocre exec update would.
- Who runs it: an ops or chief-of-staff person holding the roster and coaching hosts — half a day a month, recurring.

### Make one thing genuinely undetermined at the start
People show up live for outcomes that aren't settled yet; that's the one thing a recording structurally can't deliver. Pick one per session — a real reversible decision taken with input from the floor, or the top-upvoted questions answered unrehearsed with a visible tally of which got an answer and which got "let's take that offline."
- What has to be true: leadership will actually let something move in the room. If it's theatre it reads as theatre within two sessions.
- Failure mode: it can go badly in front of everyone, which is also why it works.
- Who runs it: whoever chairs, with enough standing to hold an exec to a real answer.

### Split the update from the gathering
Ship the news in writing beforehand, or have the room read the same short memo in silence for five minutes and spend the rest on discussion. The presentable part is exactly the substitutable part — slides are the reason the recording is as good as being there.
- What has to be true: your leadership can write. Not everyone can.
- Failure mode: people don't do pre-reads, which is why the in-room silent read is the sturdier version.
- Who runs it: whoever writes the deck today, writing prose instead.

**Cut, and why**
- Splitting into audience-specific sessions (new joiners, craft, leadership-adjacent) — right diagnosis if you've grown a lot, but it doubles production load and hardens silos. Shortlist, not a first move.
- Publishing attendance by team — it works, and buys resentment at a price you don't want when trust is already the open question.
- Making it mandatory — converts a lapsed-interest problem into a compliance problem, and you get bodies on a call with cameras off.
- Attaching it to something that already has undisputed attendance (payday, demo day, a lunch) — worth doing, but it's a scheduling fix, not an option; do it alongside whichever you pick.

**What I'd look at first, and what would change that:** the two things that reliably move this are giving people something they can't get from the recap and having someone other than the exec team do the talking — so options 2 and 3 together, which is also the cheapest pair to try. Run the skip-a-month test before any of it. If you're spread across many timezones, option 4 moves to first; if headcount roughly doubled over the period attendance slid, the shortlisted split becomes the main answer instead.
