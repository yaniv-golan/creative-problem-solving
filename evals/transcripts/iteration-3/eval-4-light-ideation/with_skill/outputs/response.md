Yes — but which ones apply depends on the cause, and four causes look identical from the turnout number. Cheap ways to split them:

- **Substitution.** The recording and notes are good enough that live is the inconvenient way to get the same thing. *Test:* recording views over the same months — if views absorb the drop, nothing is broken; your distribution changed shape.
- **No consequence.** Nothing is decided there. *Test:* ask three people who skipped to name the last thing it changed. Blank answers confirm it.
- **Mechanical.** A timezone shift, a sprint boundary, a conflict added at some point. *Test:* split turnout by team and region over the last eight sessions — this shows up as a cliff in one group, not a slope everywhere.
- **Withdrawal.** People stopped thinking it's worth speaking in. *Test:* questions asked per session. If those fell *before* turnout did, it isn't logistics.

An afternoon of pulling numbers separates them, and it changes which of the below is worth trying.

### Pause it for two cycles
Announce a pause, not a cancellation, and say nothing else. Removing the option is the only way to make latent demand visible — whoever asks for it back tells you, unprompted, what they were getting from it.
- What has to be true: enough people care that someone asks.
- Cost: if nobody asks, you have to decide in public whether to bring it back.

### Fire it on accumulated signal, not on the calendar
Monthly sets the cadence by the calendar rather than by when something actually changed. Hold it when a threshold is crossed — a set number of open decisions or submitted questions — so it always has a reason to exist that predates the invite.
- What has to be true: someone owns the queue between sessions.
- Failure mode: irregular slots are harder to defend on calendars; you reach fewer people, more of whom care.

### Put a real allocation in the room
Decide something binding live — a budget line, a headcount, a roadmap slot. Presence then buys input into an outcome rather than receipt of a summary — attendance tracks consequence far more reliably than content quality.
- What has to be true: leadership is willing to decide in front of everyone rather than stage a decision already made — people can tell instantly.
- Cost: some decisions get worse when made in a room of 200.

### Make the recording deliberately worse than the room
Publish the numbers and decisions in writing, and stop recording the unscripted part. The artifact currently competes with the event and wins on convenience; this removes the substitute instead of trying to out-compete it.
- What has to be true: something worth being present for is actually said there. Otherwise it reads as manipulation and costs trust.

**Cut:** mandatory attendance (converts absence into resentful presence and destroys your only honest signal); a shorter, tighter format (the default answer with a stopwatch); rotating presenters (a content fix for what may be a consequence problem).

**One reframe:** turnout is a poor proxy for shared context — a full room can hold none. Sampling five people a week later on where the company stands tells you more than the count does.

**What would change my ranking:** whether the missing half is concentrated in one team, region or level, or spread evenly — concentrated points at a mechanical or manager-level cause and most of the above is overkill. And whether your most senior people are among the missing. If so, this is a trust question wearing a scheduling costume.
