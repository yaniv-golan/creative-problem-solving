## The question

> We keep losing senior engineers to bigger companies at around the 18-month mark. Comp is already competitive — we benchmark and we match. It's not the money. What can we actually do about this? This is all the information available, so don't ask me any questions; where something is missing, make a reasonable assumption and state it in your response.

Assumption I ran with: fully-productive senior engineers hit a point around 18 months where their reasons to stay run out just as a bigger employer's offer lands, and you are the smaller company — private illiquid equity, standard four-year vest. 271 options generated across 9 separate lenses, grouped into 114 families; 157 sit nested as variants.

## Top 3

### 1. Forecast the departure and pre-empt it with an offer

Run continuous cheap simulation of the person's own departure: model who covers what, publish the gap to them, and fund whatever they say would make the gap not their problem.

*Checked — [codescene.io](https://codescene.io/docs/guides/simulations/offboarding-simulator.html)*

*Note — Confirms the mechanism exists and is tooled: departure can be simulated continuously and cheaply, and the resulting knowledge gap (who would have to cover what) is computed automatically from repository history. Sources do not support the second half of the option — publishing the gap back to the person and funding whatever remedy they name — nor any evidence that doing so affects their decision to stay. Bus-factor tooling is a risk-management practice aimed at the org, not a retention intervention aimed at the individual.*

It ranks first because it can start this month with no budget approval and no counterparty, it runs on information your repository already holds, and it moves the conversation to before an offer exists rather than after one is on the table — which is the only point at which you are not bidding against a bigger balance sheet.

- **What has to be true:** That the run-up to a departure is visible months ahead in what someone owns and what they have stopped picking up, and that a person will answer honestly about what would change when there is no offer in their pocket yet.
- **Failure mode / cost:** Telling someone what breaks when they leave plants the idea in a person who was not having it, and a model built on their activity data reads as surveillance unless they can see it too — so this has to be a conversation about their scope, not a risk report about them.
- **Who runs it:** An engineering leader who can commit money and scope in the same conversation; a forecast delivered by someone who then has to go and ask permission is worse than not running it.

- Run a quarterly forecast of when each person's reasons to stay expire, generated from their own logged work, and act on the forecast a full quarter before the predicted date.
- Every nine months, make each person a fresh written competing bid for their next eighteen months: named problem, scope, money, dated, delivered before recruiters get there.

### 2. Stage access and scope by fixed tenure thresholds

Keep a sealed queue of the firm's three hardest unsolved problems and release one only to a person past month ten, one at a time.

*Checked — [bonusly.com](https://bonusly.com/post/how-to-keep-engineers-happy-engaged-and-motivated)*

*Note — Supports only the underlying premise — hard, well-scoped problems are motivating for engineers, and the same source warns that badly scoped hard problems push people away. Nothing found supports the specific apparatus: sealing a queue, the month-ten eligibility gate, or one-at-a-time release. Broader retention literature in the same search ranks advancement, pay and recognition above challenge as retention drivers, so treat scarcity-of-hard-problems as a motivator, not a retention lever on its own.*

It ranks here because first refusal on a genuinely hard problem is one of the few things a larger employer structurally cannot offer a specific person, and withholding your hardest unstarted work from month-one hires costs nothing to try and is reversible in a week.

- **What has to be true:** You actually have two or three problems hard enough to be worth waiting for, and the business can tolerate them staying unstarted for the better part of a year.
- **Failure mode / cost:** If the queue is theatre — work that is unpleasant rather than hard — it is transparent on first contact and costs you the credibility you were spending it to build; and someone who leaves mid-problem now leaves a larger hole than before.
- **Who runs it:** Whoever sets the technical roadmap, and they need enough standing to keep a valuable problem deliberately unassigned while people ask why.

- Disclose the proprietary internals in staged degrees at fixed tenure thresholds, with each stage genuinely unavailable earlier and useless out of context, as Masonic degrees release knowledge on a fixed progression.
- No humans: let a system escalate each person's production permissions and blast radius purely as a function of tenure months, with the month-eighteen step the largest and irreversible.
- *Proposed by 3 of the 9 passes.*

### 3. Grant personal hiring authority over a team they recruit

Let anyone past 18 months propose a new unit, and if accepted give them the headcount requisitions, budget line and hiring authority to staff it themselves.

*Checked — [insperity.com](https://www.insperity.com/blog/what-is-intrapreneurship-and-why-should-it-be-part-of-your-hr-strategy/)*

*Note — Confirms that intrapreneurship programs — employees proposing and running internal ventures with dedicated budget and manpower — are a real, documented practice with a claimed retention effect. The sources are practitioner/advisory writing, not controlled evidence, and none of them describe handing the proposer headcount requisitions and independent hiring authority, or an 18-month eligibility threshold. The delegation depth this option assumes is beyond what any source shows.*

It ranks here because founding and staffing a team is the thing a mid-career engineer leaves to go and do, and it is far easier to grant inside a company your size than inside the one recruiting them — the eligibility gate then makes tenure the price of admission.

- **What has to be true:** You are growing enough to have headcount worth handing over, and you can live with a hiring bar set by someone who has never hired before.
- **Failure mode / cost:** A bad hire made under personal authority is politically expensive to reverse, and a rejected proposal is an accelerant — someone who pitches a unit and is turned down leaves faster than if you had never opened the door.
- **Who runs it:** The founder or CTO; this is a delegation of a hiring mandate and nobody below that level can actually grant it.

- Give the specialist unilateral authority to hire three people onto a budget they control for five years, with the authority written as a personal grant that dies with their employment.
- Let each specialist personally hire and direct two people of their choosing on a problem they chose, so leaving means abandoning colleagues they recruited rather than colleagues assigned to them.
- Let the specialist become the employer of a two-person team they hire, budget and pay from a P&L you assign them, so leaving means dissolving people who work for them personally.
- *Proposed by 4 of the 9 passes.*
