Falling all-hands attendance is almost always a symptom, not the problem. People skip when the meeting stops being the cheapest way to learn something they care about. So before adding a mandate or a trivia round, it's worth knowing which failure you actually have.

**Quick diagnosis — three very different causes**

1. **Low information value.** Everything said is already in Slack, the deck, or the weekly email. Skipping costs nothing.
2. **Low stakes / no consequence.** Nothing decided, nothing changes, no one notices absence. It reads as optional because it is.
3. **Friction.** Bad time zone, mid-sprint slot, back-to-back with something heavier, recording always available so live attendance is strictly dominated.

Cheapest way to tell them apart: look at *who* is missing. If it's clustered by team or time zone, it's friction. If it's spread evenly and skews senior, it's value. If newer people attend and long-tenured people don't, it's "I've seen this movie ten times" — also value, but the fix is different.

**Ideas, roughly in order of effort**

- **Ask, but not in a survey.** Five 10-minute calls with people who've stopped coming will tell you more than a company-wide form, which mostly collects polite non-answers.
- **Move the news out, keep the meeting for the things that only work live.** Metrics, launches, and headcount updates go in a written pre-read. The 30 minutes live are then reserved for what a doc can't do: unscripted Q&A, a real decision being explained by the person who made it, a customer story, a demo that could break.
- **Put something at stake.** A live decision, a genuinely contested tradeoff, a leader answering the uncomfortable question that's been circulating. People show up when they suspect they'll hear something they can't get secondhand.
- **Unfiltered Q&A with real teeth.** Anonymous submissions, upvoted, top questions answered live — including the ones leadership would rather skip. If the top-voted question gets dodged twice, attendance is gone for a year, so this only works if you commit.
- **Shrink it.** 60 minutes is often 25 minutes of content. Cut to 30 and it becomes hard to justify skipping.
- **Rotate the stage.** Let ICs and teams present their own work rather than a fixed exec lineup. Attendance rises partly because people come to see their peers, and partly because presenters bring their teams.
- **Try a different cadence.** Monthly is an awkward interval — too infrequent for momentum, too frequent to feel special. Quarterly-but-excellent plus a strong written monthly update beats a mediocre monthly ritual. This is worth considering seriously rather than treating cadence as fixed.
- **Delay the recording.** Not a trick to force attendance, but if the recording is posted instantly, live attendance has no advantage. Doing live-only Q&A creates a real reason to be there.

**What I'd avoid**

Mandating attendance or tracking it publicly. It converts a value problem into a compliance problem — you'll get bodies with laptops open and cameras off, and you'll lose the signal that attendance was giving you. Same with adding games or prizes: they paper over a meeting people don't need. Attendance is a useful thermometer; don't break the thermometer.

**If you want one move**

Run the next one at half the length, with a written pre-read replacing all status content, and the entire live block as upvoted anonymous Q&A. It's a single-meeting experiment, it's reversible, and the attendance delta tells you whether the problem was value or friction.
