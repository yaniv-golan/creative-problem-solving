I'm reading this as a live session people join and "half" as live attendance, not counting people who watch a recording afterwards. If a big chunk are watching later, this is a much smaller problem than it looks.

**Worth knowing before you pick a fix: is the drop uniform or concentrated?** Pull the last six months of attendance split by team, timezone, and tenure. A slide that's even across everyone points at the content — nothing happens in that hour that you can't get from the recap. A drop concentrated in a few teams usually means a standing collision (a customer call, a shift, an awkward hour) or that those teams' leads quietly stopped coming, which everyone below them reads as permission. A drop that tracks tenure means the room stopped feeling like "us" as headcount grew.

The thing every angle on this kept landing on: nothing that currently happens in that hour requires being in the hour. Until something does, better slides won't hold anyone.

### Make the room the cheapest place to meet someone from another team

Open with four minutes of randomised breakouts of six, deliberately mixed across teams, with one throwaway prompt. The value of showing up stops depending on the content and starts scaling with who else is there — which is the one thing a recording structurally cannot give you, and the reason people show up to conferences they think are mediocre.

- What has to be true: enough people are strangers to each other that meeting someone is worth four minutes. Below ~40 people this does nothing.
- Cost: it's the segment people will complain about loudest for the first two months, and it dies instantly if leadership treats it as the skippable part.

### Give the slot a price and a real producer

Publish the fully-loaded cost of the hour — headcount × an average hourly rate, it doesn't need to be precise — and treat it as a budget somebody spends and reports back on. Paired rule: if there's nothing that clears the bar, cancel that month. Right now whoever runs it bears no cost for a weak session, so the quality floor never rises; and a session that can be cancelled is one where turning up implies something actually happened.

- What has to be true: you're willing to actually cancel one. Announcing the rule and never using it is worse than not having it.
- Cost: it can tip into theatre, and it puts a named person on the hook for a number they only partly control.

### Unbundle it — write the update, keep only what can't be written

Everything that is a status report becomes a written brief, ideally generated from the systems you already have (shipped work, pipeline, hiring, cash). The live slot keeps only the two things that don't survive being written down: unvetted questions answered in real time, and one decision that is genuinely still open being argued in front of people. Add a standing channel where anyone can ask any exec anything in public and get a public answer inside 72 hours, so the demand stops being artificially bunched into a monthly window.

- What has to be true: your execs can take an unscreened question in public without it becoming a comms incident. If they can't, this makes things worse, and that's its own finding.
- Cost: writing a good brief every month is real work, and the honest version of this shrinks the meeting to about 20 minutes.

### Put the teams on stage and the execs in the audience

Rotate so each team presents its own work to the company a couple of times a year, with leadership responding in the room rather than presenting. Obligation becomes person-level rather than org-level — you come when your own team is up, when the team you depend on is up, and eventually because your peers' work is the content instead of a leadership broadcast.

- What has to be true: someone coaches the presenting teams, or the first three sessions are worse than what you have now and you'll lose the room.
- Cost: it can drift into internal marketing, where teams present the polished version and nothing true gets said.

### Stop counting attendance and count what it was a proxy for

Attendance was never the goal — knowing where the company is going and being able to ask about it was. Replace the number with a four-question quarterly pulse (can you name the top three priorities, do you know where your work fits, what did you want to ask and didn't) plus a one-click "not coming, because…" on the invite that routes reasons to whoever owns the session. You either discover this isn't a problem, or you get a list of specific reasons instead of a trendline.

- What has to be true: you can accept an answer like "70% is fine" and stop working on it.
- Cost: it feels like moving the goalposts, and it will read that way to anyone who's been pushing on attendance.

**Cut:** mandating attendance or having managers chase it (moves the number, not the cause); a no-recording rule or a "send a delegate" rota (taxes exactly the people with the least schedule control); and killing the session with nothing to replace it, which just relocates the confusion.
