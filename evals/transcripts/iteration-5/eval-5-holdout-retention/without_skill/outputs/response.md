# Losing senior engineers at 18 months

## First, the 18-month number is a clue, not noise

Attrition that clusters that tightly is structural, not a series of individual bad experiences. Two things are almost certainly happening:

**The decision is made around month 11–14 and executed at 18.** Search, interview loops, notice periods, and the internal "am I really doing this" phase run 4–7 months for a senior person. Which means whatever causes this is happening in months 9–14 — and your intervention window is month 6–12, not month 17 with a counteroffer.

**The one-year cliff has cleared.** They're no longer forfeiting anything by leaving, and they're at peak marketability: enough tenure to look committed, not so much that a move looks like churn. Recruiter contact rates peak in exactly this window.

## Second: I'd push back gently on "it's not the money"

You benchmark and match. But most benchmarking compares *year-one* offers, and big companies don't compete on year one — they compete on years two through four, via annual refresh grants. Someone at Google or Meta gets a new RSU grant every year stacked on top of the old one. If your senior engineers got one grant at hire and nothing since, their effective total comp is *declining* relative to market every single year they stay, even though you "matched" on day one.

**Concrete check you can do this week:** model 3-year total comp for one of your seniors — cash plus realistic equity value — against a 3-year model of the offer they'd get at the company that just poached them. Include the big company's annual refreshers. If you're 20–40% behind by year three, you don't have a mystery, you have a comp structure that's blind at the point where it matters.

That's not the whole story, but it's the cheapest thing to rule out, and it's the one most companies get wrong while sincerely believing they pay competitively.

## Third: diagnose before you prescribe

Exit interviews are close to worthless here — people are diplomatic, they want the reference, and they've already rationalized the decision. Three better instruments:

**Triangulate on destination, not stated reason.** Same "went to a bigger company" outcome, three very different diagnoses:
- Went to bigger *scope* (larger systems, harder problems) → you have a **scope ceiling**
- Went to a *calmer* job with the same scope → you have a **burnout / friction** problem
- Went to a more *prestigious name* at similar scope → you have a **belief and career-risk** problem, i.e. they stopped believing the equity was real

**Interview the stayers, not the leavers.** Talk to everyone past 30 months. What's structurally different about their situation? Usually it's one of three things: they report to a specific manager, they own a distinct area outright, or they got a real promotion. That tells you what's working, and it's more honest data than an exit interview.

**Check whether it's concentrated.** Attrition is rarely evenly distributed. If four of your last six leavers reported to the same two managers, you don't have a retention problem — you have a management problem wearing a retention costume. Run this before anything else; it's a 20-minute spreadsheet exercise and it changes everything downstream.

**Call the alumni at 6–12 months out.** They're candid once they're settled and there's nothing at stake. Don't ask "why did you leave" — ask "when did you *first* seriously think about leaving, and what was happening that month?" That question gets you the actual trigger event, which is usually something specific: a reorg, a shelved project, a promotion that went to someone else, a manager change.

## What's usually actually going on at a well-paying startup

In rough order of likelihood:

1. **No visible next rung.** At a 40–150 person company, a senior engineer can see the entire org chart and there is no one above them to become. No staff track, no principal track, nobody more senior to learn from. This is the most common cause and the most fixable.

2. **The problem stopped being hard.** Senior engineers are motivated by novel difficulty more than by title. They arrived, absorbed your hardest problem, solved it — and by month 18 the roadmap ahead looks like variations on a solved theme. Big companies offer bigger *problems*, not just bigger paychecks.

3. **Career-risk hedging.** Illiquid equity, a valuation they privately suspect is optimistic, and a mortgage. Banking a recognizable name and liquid RSUs is the rational move. This is a *belief* problem, and vague or evasive equity communication makes it dramatically worse.

4. **Accumulated friction they were promised would get fixed.** They arrived, found the tooling/codebase/process rough, were told "we'll get to that." At 18 months they've watched it not get fixed and concluded it never will.

## What actually moves the needle

**Structural, do these:**
- **Annual equity refresh starting at month 12** — not at the 4-year cliff. Most companies treat refresh as a year-4 problem. It's a year-2 problem. This is the single highest-leverage fix.
- **Build a real staff/principal IC track and promote 1–2 people into it visibly.** Publish the expectations. The signal that a next rung exists matters nearly as much as the rung.
- **Secondary liquidity at the next round for anyone past 24 months.** Even $50–100K resets the "my equity is monopoly money" calculus completely. Nothing else converts skepticism into commitment as fast.

**Operational, start this quarter:**
- **Stay interviews at month 9**, run by someone who isn't their manager. Three questions: what would make you leave; what's the most interesting problem here you're not allowed to work on; who here do you learn from. That last one is a great early-warning signal — "nobody" means you have about nine months.
- **Maintain a "hard problems" list and give seniors first claim on it.** Manufacture novel difficulty deliberately rather than hoping the roadmap supplies it.
- **Put senior engineers in the room where strategy is decided.** This is the one thing a big company structurally cannot offer — proximity to consequence — and most startups squander it by treating engineers as ticket-takers. It's your actual competitive advantage against Google.
- **Sponsor external visibility** — conference talks, open source, writing. Counterintuitive because it makes them more marketable, but the FAANG move is often really buying *legitimacy*, and you can supply that in-house.

**Don't bother with:**
- **Counteroffers at month 18.** They mostly fail within a year, and they teach the org that resigning is the way to get paid.
- **More perks, offsites, culture programming.** If the problem is scope or growth, this reads as insulting.

## One reframe worth sitting with

Some of this may be fine. A startup that hires senior people, gives them the best two years of their career, and sends them off as alumni is not automatically broken. The real question is *which* seniors you're losing. If you're losing the median and keeping your top three, the system is working. If it's the inverse, that's an emergency and everything above becomes urgent.

It may also be more honest to design for a realistic 3-year tenure — with deliberate knowledge capture, documented ownership, and a strong alumni network — than to chase a 6-year tenure you're unlikely to get in this market.

**If you do only three things:** run the manager-concentration check, model 3-year total comp against a real competing offer, and start stay interviews at month 9.
