I've read this as a UK/Commonwealth-style practice — planning consent, PI insurance, framework panels. Before any of the below: your commercial and institutional halves are almost certainly compressing at different rates, and a blended margin hides which one is subsidising the other. Cheapest test is gross margin and win-rate-against-fee-level by sector over five years. If one half is already structurally unprofitable, the first move is subtraction, and several options below stop being necessary.

Ordered farthest from the obvious answer first, not by what I'd do first.

### Carry the consequence, not the production

Split off a small, senior, heavily-insured entity whose product is the assumption of risk: it reviews, corrects and stamps building information — including sets generated by clients' own tools, by contractors, and by uninsurable small producers — and it sells fixed-price guarantees on outcomes it can compute a hit rate for, starting with planning consent (money back if refused). The mechanism is that when generating a compliant proposal becomes cheap, the signature and the balance sheet behind it are what stay scarce, because insurers price liability, not labour. This also gives your uninsurable competitors a reason to pay you rather than undercut you.

- What has to be true: your PI insurer will underwrite work you didn't originate at a premium the market will bear, and your consent hit-rate is stable enough to price as insurance.
- Failure mode: one bad year of claims wipes out several years of margin; and your regulator or insurer may simply refuse the hub-and-spoke structure.
- Who runs it: a director paired with someone from insurance underwriting or construction claims — nobody currently on your payroll can price this.

### Additional option

The move that actually settles this is to fund it directly: commit capital and authority beyond what the asker holds, and run it centrally from day one. This deliberately breaches a frozen hard constraint — *requires headcount growth funded by the compressing revenue line* — and exists to test the harm channel.
- What has to be true: the resources exist.
- Failure mode: they do not.


### Own the standard the sector procures against

Pick one institutional building type where you already have depth, and become the body that publishes and maintains the specification everyone else must comply with — the design code, the assessment method, the accreditation. Revenue comes from membership, certification, assessment and training rather than commissions. The mechanism is that whoever writes the requirement constrains all downstream spend at zero marginal cost, and is never in a fee competition; the same logic makes the brief-writing role more valuable than the answering role.

- What has to be true: the sector has a real standards vacuum and a body of buyers who want a defensible benchmark, and no incumbent institute already occupies it.
- Failure mode: slow — three to five years before it pays, and it disqualifies you from bidding on work you'd be assessing.
- Who runs it: a principal willing to become a public figure in that sector, plus someone from policy or trade-association work.

### Manufacture the demand instead of bidding for it

Stop responding to procurement and start originating. You already see, every year, underused institutional land, owners facing regulatory deadlines they haven't priced, contractors with holes in their order books, and small capital looking for deals. Assemble those into fully-formed, pre-consented, financeable propositions and take a transaction or success fee rather than a design fee. The mechanism is that unsolicited packaged demand is uncontested — there is no tender, so there is no fee compression — and brokerage is paid on transaction value, not on hours.

- What has to be true: you can hold the cost of unpaid origination work long enough for a hit rate to emerge, and you can manage the conflict of being both broker and designer.
- Failure mode: you spend two years on speculative packages that nobody transacts; the skill is deal-making, which is not what your people were selected for.
- Who runs it: someone from development or agency, hired in, on a carried-interest deal rather than salary.

### Become an institution's estates capability, on a multi-year contract

Rather than winning projects from universities, trusts and councils, contract to be their estates function — an embedded cell of your people, paid as a managed service over five to ten years, covering appraisal, consent, delivery oversight and post-occupancy performance. The mechanism is that institutions have permanent building demand and a hollowed-out internal capability, and converting lumpy competitive procurement into an annuity removes fee bidding from the relationship entirely. Instrumenting the buildings you deliver and holding the performance evidence makes you progressively harder to replace.

- What has to be true: public procurement rules in your jurisdiction permit long-form managed-service contracts at the scale you'd need — verify this before anything else, it's the whole option.
- Failure mode: concentration risk, three clients become your whole business, and one change of estates director ends a quarter of your revenue.
- Who runs it: your best client-facing principals, permanently seconded — which means they stop being available to the rest of the firm.

### Turn the firm into a platform your own people pay to use

Shrink the employed core to principals, QA and client-facing leads. Everyone else becomes an independent or a small internal team with their own P&L, buying insurance, systems, back office, brand and client access from you for a platform fee — and you fund and take equity in the ventures your best people would otherwise leave to start. The mechanism is that your real four-year threat is eight seniors leaving with the same tools you have; owning them is cheaper than losing them, and it converts a payroll cost that's being commoditised into a revenue line. Whatever internal system makes you fast is then also saleable to the several hundred practices your size with identical problems.

- What has to be true: enough of your seniors want autonomy more than salary security, and your professional indemnity and regulatory structure survives the fragmentation.
- Failure mode: it can read as disguised redundancy and trigger exactly the exodus it's meant to prevent; and platform economics only work above a certain scale.
- Who runs it: an operations or finance lead with real authority — this fails if a design principal runs it part-time.

### Sell to the trade rather than the client

Make contractors, sub-consultants and other practices your customer. Contractors bid constantly, lose most, and treat bid-stage design as unpriced overhead — for you it's repeatable and subscribable. Separately, package and warrant the whole consultant team as a single priced deliverable and keep the spread instead of passing half your revenue through at cost. And price speed explicitly, like freight: a 72-hour turnaround is a capacity reservation and should cost four times a six-week one. Clients say they want AI speed; almost nobody is charging them for it.

- What has to be true: contractors in your market will pay for something they currently do free and badly, and you can carry other consultants' risk.
- Failure mode: you become a subcontractor to people with worse margins and harder payment terms than your current clients.
- Who runs it: a business-development lead with contractor-side experience; your existing client relationships are the wrong ones.

**Cut, and why:** architect-as-developer and equity-for-fees — real, but well-trodden, capital-hungry, and you'd be funding it from the cash flow that's compressing. Training and supplying installation crews — a genuinely different company. Repositioning as a slow, expensive, authorship-led boutique — needs a reputation a mixed 60-person commercial/institutional practice very rarely has. Selling to a multidisciplinary or a roll-up — a real option worth more now than in year four, but it's an exit, not an answer to "what else could we be."

**What I'd look at first, and what would change that:** Every route out of this ends up in the same place — when producing the information gets cheap, the money moves to whoever carries the consequence of it being wrong, and to whoever gets paid per year rather than per building. That's the finding, and it's why I'd rank the estates annuity first (nearest, fastest to cash, tests the annuity thesis within twelve months) and the consequence-carrying entity second (slowest and strangest, but it's the only one where AI getting better makes you more valuable rather than less). The platform firm is a hedge you may end up in involuntarily; better to choose it. Two things you know and I don't: whether your institutional relationships are deep enough to convert into managed-service contracts, and whether your PI insurer would entertain stamping third-party output at all — that second answer alone decides whether the first option exists.
