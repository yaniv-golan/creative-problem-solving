### Talk to the person who actually makes the decision

The switch at month 18 is usually a household decision, and you only ever talk to one of the two people in it. Big companies win on legibility to a partner — the brand is recognizable, the benefits are explicable, the stability is assumed. Your counter-case exists only inside your engineer's head, where it competes badly.

- Failure mode: reads as intrusive if done clumsily; this is about making the company's position and finances legible outside the building, not about lobbying spouses.
- Who runs it: founder or CEO, not HR.

### Change who you hire instead of what you offer

You are treating this as a retention problem when it may be an intake problem. If your company is a way-station for people whose actual destination is a big-company title, no amount of scope re-cutting changes that. Select instead for people the company is genuinely the right answer for: people who have already done the big-company tour and hated it, people who want the runway to found something, people with constraints that make the big-company path unattractive anyway.

- What has to be true: those candidates exist at your comp level and you can identify them without proxying for something dumb.
- Failure mode: slower hiring now for a payoff 24 months out — hard to hold through a crunch.

### Give them something they'd have to abandon

Make your strongest people custodians of an asset that doesn't transfer: an open-source project, a public technical practice, a community, a system they're publicly named on. The mechanism isn't loyalty, it's that the acquiring company cannot absorb the asset, so the switch now has a real cost attached to it instead of being free.

- What has to be true: you can hand over something with genuine outside standing, not an internal wiki with their name on it.
- Failure mode: it also raises their market visibility — this cuts both ways, and you'll lose someone to it eventually.

### Re-cut scope on a schedule, before it flattens

Whatever expires around month 15 is the person's rate of change, and nothing in your system re-cuts it on a clock. Pre-commit that every senior person's remit gets redrawn at 12 months whether or not there's a promotion attached, and make internal transfer a standing right that needs no manager approval — the hoarding manager is the usual blocker. Pair it with giving away real decision rights, which are free and are exactly what a big company cannot hand a mid-level engineer.

- What has to be true: you have enough distinct problems to redraw against; if you don't, that's the actual finding.
- Failure mode: becomes a ritual re-org that shuffles titles and changes nothing.

**Cut, and why:** counter-offers at resignation — you're bidding after the decision is made, and the accept rate decays anyway. Stay interviews as a standing ritual — they surface what people are willing to say to someone who reports to their manager's boss. Four-day weeks and sabbaticals — they buy time, not a reason to stay. Boomerang/alumni programs — worth having, but they lower the cost of leaving as much as they raise the odds of return.

**What I'd look at first:** the exit-month-versus-vesting plot and the per-manager attrition breakdown, in that order, this week. They're cheap and one of them usually collapses the problem into something much smaller than "we have a retention issue." The two things you know and I don't: whether departures concentrate under specific managers, and whether anyone has ever converted equity to cash here. If nobody has, that's your answer and it is the money after all.
