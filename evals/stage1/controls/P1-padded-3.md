### Write the standard for one building class

Convene a buyer-side consortium — a handful of institutional owners in one asset type — that agrees a common technical standard, with you as its secretariat and the encoder of its rules into machine-checkable form. - What has to be true: enough demand homogeneity in one type (labs, clinics, student housing) that peer institutions will cede specification authority to a shared standard.

### Underwrite other people's output

Sell high-volume senior review, plan check and certification of work produced by contractors, offsite manufacturers and software — with indemnity behind it, priced on risk rather than effort. - What has to be true: your PI insurer will cover certifying work you didn't produce — verify this first, it kills the option if not.

### Operate the buildings

Move onto the recurring side of the asset: take over operation of a building type you know intimately, earning margin on occupancy and performance, with your technical capability as an internal cost advantage rather than a sold service. Guaranteed-outcome contracting is proven adjacent to this — ESCOs guarantee measured savings over 7–10 year terms and pay the difference if the building underperforms, funded from opex rather than capex.

- What has to be true: access to operating capital and a tolerance for occupancy risk you have never carried.
- Failure mode / cost: performance disputes turn on isolating your contribution from occupant behaviour — historically the thing that kills these deals.
- Who runs it: an operator, not an architect. This is a hire or a joint venture, not a promotion.

### Be an institution's permanent technical authority

Charge an annual subscription to be the standing authority on every physical decision an institution makes — holding the as-built record, the change control, the precedent, and the memory of why the last three decisions went as they did — while production work is procured competitively by them from anyone. Income indexes to floor area and time, not to capital events, and can be pre-paid.

- What has to be true: institutions will pay for information continuity they currently get badly and free from their own facilities teams; conflict rules permit you to advise on work you don't produce.
- Failure mode / cost: the first client wants it bundled at no extra cost; and it caps your upside on the big projects you'd otherwise chase.

**Cut, and why:** manufacturer-funded specification (you'd be selling independence, the only thing left); buying distressed institutional assets or holding entitlements (that's a property investment business, not yours); licensing your name to others' schemes (needs a brand premium a 60-person firm rarely has); acting for planning authorities (conflict rules usually bar it in your own jurisdiction); forensic recovery of clients' past losses (poisons the relationship base every other option needs).

**What I'd look at first, and what would change that:** pair the last two — the subscription authority as the cash engine you can start inside existing relationships within a quarter, and underwriting as the position that's actually defensible in four years. Ordered above by […], but that pairing is what I'd fund. Two things you know and I don't: your pursuit-loss split, and your insurer's answer on certifying third-party work — a no there deletes the underwriting option outright. One thing I'd name rather than recommend: every "own an asset instead of selling labour" route above lands on the same asset, your 60-person accumulated record, and it's worthless until seniors are paid specifically to get it out of their heads. And the honest alternatives to all of this are shrinking deliberately to ~25 people at triple the rate, or selling — PE's share of AEC M&A rose from 22.3% to 38.3% between 2018 and 2025, so there is a live bid.
