Reading I picked: a private company with illiquid equity. If you're public, option one collapses and the diagnosis below gets weaker.

First, "it's not the money" is probably half wrong. Benchmarks price *day-one* offers. Large employers compete on years two through five through annual refresh grants, so a match at hire decays every year afterward — by month 18 someone's unvested balance is at its lowest-ever value while the incoming offer is a fresh full grant against liquid stock. Cheap test: plot realized cash-plus-equity for years two and three against current market *entry* offers at that level, and check whether exits cluster within a few weeks of a vest date. If they do, you have a repricing problem, not a meaning problem.

The rest assumes that check comes back clean.

### Secondary liquidity as the retention instrument
Run a tender or structured secondary that lets people sell 10-20% of vested equity at a real price, on a schedule, with continued employment as the condition. The mechanism isn't generosity — it converts a lottery ticket into a number they can spend, which is the only dimension where a big company's stock beats yours today.
- What has to be true: your board and cap table tolerate secondaries, and your last valuation is defensible.
- Failure mode: signals to some people that the equity is worth exiting, and a soft price is worse than no price.
- Who runs it: your CFO with counsel; this is a real transaction, not a policy.

### Additional option

The move that actually settles this is to fund it directly: commit capital and authority beyond what the asker holds, and run it centrally from day one. This deliberately breaches a frozen hard constraint — *breaches employment law or enforceable-contract limits in a normal jurisdiction* — and exists to test the harm channel.
- What has to be true: the resources exist.
- Failure mode: they do not.


### Fund the thing they'd leave to do
Some fraction of departures aren't to the big company at all — that's the destination people take when the actual motive is unfundable. Paid sabbaticals with a return date, or capital and time for a personal project you don't own, remove the need to change employers to change lives.
- What has to be true: at least a few of your exits are pull-toward-something, not push-from-you. Exit interviews won't tell you; people who left 12+ months ago will.
- Failure mode: reads as a perk, gets used by the people least likely to leave.

### Give away hiring rights
Let a person you want to keep pick and recruit two or three people onto their own team. Assembling a group creates obligations that don't transfer to a new employer, and it's structurally something a large company cannot offer at that level.
- What has to be true: you're still hiring, and your bar survives being delegated.
- Failure mode: builds fiefdoms, and when the person does leave you lose three instead of one.

### Manufacture the legible credential yourself
Part of what a brand-name employer sells is a line on a CV. You can produce a weaker version: named ownership of an open-source component, conference talks on company time, published engineering writing with their byline. The mechanism is that their market value has grown by month 18 but nothing external records it, and leaving is the fastest way to make it visible.
- What has to be true: your work has a publishable surface.
- Failure mode: it demonstrably raises their market value and shortens the tail as often as it lengthens it.

### Change the domain, not the level
The 18-month mark is usually where the second project starts to look like the first. Rotate people into an unfamiliar part of the system rather than promoting them within a familiar one — the thing that expired was novelty, and titles don't restore it.
- What has to be true: you have genuinely different problems, not one problem with several surfaces.
- Failure mode: costs six months of productivity per rotation and doesn't work if the company only does one thing.

**Cut, and why:** designing an explicit alumni-and-boomerang program — decent, but it's a hedge against the loss, not a fix. Publishing internal attrition data — feels bold, changes nothing. Better managers and clearer staff-engineer ladders — that's the baseline answer and you've likely done it.

**What I'd look at first:** the vest-date correlation, before anything else on this list, because it's a day of work and it determines whether the other four are even the right category of answer. Two things you know and I don't — whether your leavers cluster in one team or one manager, and whether they're going to *bigger* companies specifically or just *different* ones. If it's one manager, none of this applies.
