Assuming a UK-style market, roughly £6–8M of fee income, margins somewhere in the 5–12% band, and pricing that's still mostly percentage-of-construction-cost or time-charge with utilisation targets in the high 60s. Adjust the specifics if that's wrong, but the diagnosis probably holds either way.

The reason utilisation and cheaper juniors haven't worked is that both are leverage plays on exactly the thing that's being commoditised. Your margin engine has always been the gap between what a junior costs and what their hours bill at, applied to the production of documentation. AI compresses that gap from both ends — it makes the documentation cheaper to produce, and it removes the arbitrage on the people producing it. Pushing harder on utilisation just means selling more hours of a thing whose price is falling. Worse, under time-based pricing every efficiency gain you make hands the saving straight to the client. You get faster and you bill less. That's the trap, and it's structural rather than a matter of effort.

So the strategic question isn't how to deliver drawings more cheaply. It's what you sell instead of drawings.

There are really only a handful of durable answers, and they differ mostly in where they sit relative to the design phase. **Upstream**, you sell judgment before anyone is appointed: site capacity, planning risk, portfolio and estate strategy, development appraisal, "should this even be built and in what form." Fees here aren't benchmarked against construction cost, the work is judgment-heavy and locally specific, and it's the phase where the largest sums are decided. It's also the least automatable thing you do, because the value is in knowing which regulator, which precedent, and which failure mode — not in generating options. **Downstream**, you sell continuity: retrofit, decarbonisation, compliance and estate management as a standing retainer for institutional clients who own portfolios and have obligations that recur annually. Universities, health trusts, local authorities and large charities all have this problem and most of them are buying it project-by-project because nobody has offered them the alternative. **Sideways**, you productise: pick one or two typologies you already do well and turn them into a repeatable system — a kit of parts, a configured design platform, a licensed template — where your margin comes from reuse rather than from hours. And **around the edges** there's specialisation deep enough that you become the default answer to a narrow, regulation-heavy, liability-laden problem: building safety and gateway compliance, labs and GMP, heritage retrofit, embodied carbon. Regulation creates demand that can't be competed away on price, and liability makes clients pay for certainty rather than speed.

The options I'd be more sceptical of, given a 60-person practice: taking equity positions or co-developing needs capital and a risk appetite you probably don't have on the balance sheet, and it converts a fee business into a cyclical one at the worst possible moment. Building and selling software to other architects is a graveyard. And merging into a multidisciplinary is a real option, but it's worth much more if you do it from a position of visible differentiation than if you do it in year three when the margin decline is on the page.

If I had to converge on one shape, it's a two-engine practice. The front engine is a small, senior advisory group — six to ten people, your best judgment, selling upstream strategy and feasibility on fixed or retained fees with no relationship to construction value. It's low-headcount, high-margin, and it originates the work for everything else. The back engine is productised delivery in one or two typologies, run by fewer, more experienced people who direct and verify AI output rather than produce it, priced as a fixed fee per unit or per building rather than per hour. The current mixed workload funds the transition and gradually gets triaged: anything that's genuinely a commodity either gets delivered through the productised engine at the new cost base, or gets declined.

Two consequences follow that are harder than the strategy itself. The first is pricing. If you keep billing time while getting faster, none of this works — you have to move fixed-fee, and that means getting good at scoping and at holding the line when clients ask what your day rate is. Expect to lose some arguments and some clients in the first year. The second is the shape of the pyramid. You need fewer juniors, but not zero, because in seven years your seniors have to come from somewhere and nobody else is training them either. The answer is to hire a third as many and train them on judgment and verification from the start rather than on production — which is a genuinely different apprenticeship and one you'll have to design, since the profession's existing one assumes years of drafting.

On sequencing, the first six months are about proving the advisory offer exists: name it, price it, and sell it to three existing institutional clients who already trust you, because they're the cheapest place to test whether anyone will pay for judgment separately from drawings. The following year is picking the typology and building the productised delivery capability behind a real project, not as an R&D exercise. By month twenty-four you want a materially different revenue mix, and the metric that tells you whether it's working is the share of fee income that isn't a function of hours worked — plus revenue per head, which should be rising while utilisation falls. If you're still managing the practice on utilisation in two years, the transition hasn't happened.

The uncomfortable part is that this is a smaller practice in headcount terms, probably 40 to 45 people, earning more. Most firms in your position can't get there because the partner group's identity and the org chart are both built around volume of delivery. That, rather than AI or fee compression, is usually what decides whether the next four years work.
