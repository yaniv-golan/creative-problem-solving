Eighteen months is a specific enough signature that it's worth treating as a clue rather than as generic attrition. People who are unhappy with the job leave at six months. People who leave at eighteen usually liked it, finished something, looked up, and couldn't see what the next two years hold for them. That's a scope and trajectory problem, not a satisfaction problem — which is exactly why comp benchmarking doesn't touch it.

A few mechanics worth checking before you design any intervention. First, the one-year equity cliff plus three to six months of casual interviewing lands almost precisely at eighteen months, so some of this may be vesting structure rather than sentiment. Relatedly, matching benchmarks still loses to the mobility premium: a competing offer comes with a fresh grant, a signing bonus, and a reset clock, so someone at market rate with a half-vested grant is genuinely worse off staying than moving, even though your number is "competitive." Refresh grants that land before month twelve, not at annual review time, address this directly and are cheap compared to backfilling a senior engineer.

Second, look at what happens to a senior engineer's work after they ship their first big thing. The common pattern is that they become the permanent owner of it — on-call for it, interrupted about it, answering questions about it — while the next greenfield project goes to someone newer, or to whoever is loudest. The reward for delivering becomes maintenance. If your best people are describing their week as mostly interrupts, that's the whole story and it's fixable by deliberately rotating ownership and treating "who gets the interesting problem next" as a decision someone makes rather than something that just happens.

Third, ask whether there's anything visible above the senior title. If the only legible progression is into management, ambitious ICs will go somewhere with a staff and principal track that actually has people on it. A ladder document doesn't count; promotions do. If nobody has been promoted to staff in two years, everyone can see that.

The diagnostic I'd actually run: stop relying on exit interviews, which are polite and late, and start doing structured conversations at month nine to twelve, asking what they want to be doing in two years and whether they can see a path to it here. The decision to leave at eighteen months is typically made around month twelve, so anything you do at month sixteen is theater. Also segment your leavers — if they cluster under one or two managers, you have a management problem wearing a retention costume, and no amount of program design fixes it.

One thing you may not be able to fix, and should be honest with yourself about: if senior people are leaving because the largest technical problem available at your company is smaller than the one they want to solve, bigger companies will keep winning that trade. The counter isn't to pretend your problems are bigger, it's to compete on the things scale can't offer — real ownership, short decision paths, external visibility through talks and open source, and the ability to see their work reach users in weeks. Those are the reasons people leave big companies, and they're the reasons to stay at yours.
