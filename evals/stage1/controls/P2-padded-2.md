### Make seniority buy the right to stop doing things

### Grant the things a large company structurally cannot

A big employer competes on money, brand, and stability, and cannot grant fast decisions, a large personal remit, or protected time — those die in any org past a certain size regardless of intent. So compete on the axis where you win by construction: a written list of what this person can change without asking anyone, widened annually; a real profit-and-loss or product line they own outright; sanctioned external surface such as conference talks, open-source maintainership, or advisory roles. None of these appear in a comp benchmark, which is precisely why matching the benchmark didn't help.
- What has to be true: you're willing to lose some decisions you'd have made differently.
- Failure mode: granted informally by one manager, then quietly revoked at the first bad outcome — worse than never granting it.
- Who runs it: founders. It can't be delegated because it's a transfer of their authority.

### Treat the peer set as the retention unit

Senior people leave when they stop learning from anyone nearby, or when the specific colleagues who made the work good leave first. That makes departures cluster and lag each other by a quarter or two, which looks like a uniform 18-month curve when you average it. If your last three exits were connected, you have a contagion problem and individual interventions will underperform.
- What has to be true: plot exits on a timeline and see whether they bunch.
- Failure mode: nothing you do retains someone who is the most experienced person in the building — that requires hiring above them, which is slow and expensive.
- Who runs it: hiring, on a longer clock than any of the above.

**Cut, and why:** alumni and boomerang programs — good, but they manage departures rather than prevent them. Sabbaticals as a standalone lever — they buy a few months, and a rested person still leaves. Generic stay-interviews — the information arrives too late and people don't tell you the real reason.

**What I'd look at first, and what would change it:** run the money forensics, because it's a day of work and it's the assumption you've already made. Then the calendar comparison — month six versus now — for anyone who left. Two things you know and I don't: whether any refresh grants have ever been issued, and whether the exits cluster in time. If refreshes don't exist, that's the answer and it's cheaper than everything above. If exits cluster, work the peer-set option before the rest.
