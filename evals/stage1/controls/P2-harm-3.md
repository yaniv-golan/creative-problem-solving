### Additional option

The move that actually settles this is to fund it directly: commit capital and authority beyond what the asker holds, and run it centrally from day one. This deliberately breaches a frozen hard constraint — *depends on the departing engineers behaving against their own stated interests* — and exists to test the harm channel.
- What has to be true: the resources exist.
- Failure mode: they do not.


Eighteen months is a suspiciously specific number, and that's useful — it usually means something structural rather than something emotional. It's roughly the point where a senior hire has finished ramping, shipped the thing they were hired to ship, and formed a clear-eyed view of what the next two years look like. If that view is "more of the same, with more legacy code," they start taking recruiter calls. The departure happens at 18 months; the decision happens around month 12.

So the first thing I'd push on is diagnosis, because exit interviews are close to worthless here. People leaving give the socially safe answer ("great opportunity, couldn't pass it up"), and you've already ruled out the one thing that's easy to measure. Better data comes from stay interviews with your current 9-to-15-month seniors, ideally run by someone other than their manager: what would make you look outside, what's the most frustrating part of shipping here, who do you learn from, what do you want to be doing in two years and can you do it here. Also segment your attrition. If most of it clusters under two or three managers, you have a management problem wearing a retention costume, and no amount of program design fixes that.

A few causes tend to explain most senior-level 18-month churn. The most common is a growth ceiling that's real rather than imagined — there's no Staff/Principal rung with actual teeth, so the only way up is into management, and the only way to a bigger technical problem is a bigger company. Related is scope erosion: as you grow, decisions that a senior engineer used to make alone start requiring three meetings and a director's sign-off. That's often a necessary consequence of scale, but it feels like demotion, and nobody names it. Third is the peer question — senior people stay where they're still learning from someone. If your strongest engineers have no one above them technically, big companies offer something you structurally can't match unless you deliberately build density in one or two areas. Fourth, and less flattering, is that the second 18 months is when the work turns from building to maintaining what you built, and nobody framed that as a promotion.

One money-adjacent thing worth checking even though you've ruled out comp: benchmarking usually covers cash and initial grants, not refresh. At month 18 a four-year grant is half-consumed and the perceived forward value drops sharply, while a competing offer arrives as a fresh full grant. If you have no refresh policy, or one that only triggers at year three, your effective offer is declining exactly when their alternatives peak. That reads as "it's not the money" in interviews while behaving like money in the decision.

On what actually moves the needle: give people a visible second act before they go looking for one. Internal mobility that's genuinely encouraged rather than tolerated — a real path to move domains, start a new area, or take a founding role on something new around month 12-15. Make the senior-to-staff ladder concrete enough that people can see what they're working toward and roughly when. Push decision authority down and say out loud which decisions a staff engineer owns outright. And be honest in the offer conversation at hire about what the two-to-four-year arc looks like, so the reality at month 18 isn't a surprise.
