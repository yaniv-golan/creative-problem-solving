## The diagnosis, briefly

You have correctly identified that you are dying, but the two remedies you've tried are both accelerants.

Your business model sells hours of documentation production, priced as a percentage of construction cost, with margin generated by leverage — juniors billed above their cost, supervised by seniors. That model has two independent failure points and both are firing at once.

The price is falling, because clients have learned that AI-assisted competitors will quote lower and because the fee percentage was already the softest line item in any development budget. And the leverage engine is disintegrating, because the junior production hours that generated your margin are precisely the work that AI automates first. Drafting, sheet setting, schedule coordination, code checking, option enumeration, specification assembly — that is the bottom two-thirds of your pyramid.

Raising utilisation squeezes an asset that is already dry, and it consumes the one thing that would fund a transition: slack. A practice at 85% utilisation cannot reinvent itself, because reinvention is unbillable. Hiring cheaper juniors doubles down on the input whose market value is depreciating fastest, and it does so at the exact moment when the constraint on your business is no longer production capacity at all.

Here is the pivotal point, and it is the one most practices miss. **AI does not save you. Left alone, it deflates fees for everyone in your market and you hand the entire productivity gain to your clients.** If you sell effort and your effort halves, your revenue halves. The only way to capture the value of a productivity gain is to change what you are selling — from effort to outcome, or from effort to an asset you own. Every viable version of your future runs through that sentence.

So at the highest level there are three strategic responses: be cheaper, be different, or be closer to the money. You have tried cheaper and correctly concluded it is a losing race. The rest of this note is about the other two.

## Six things you could be (restated)

### 1. The typology specialist with a productised delivery system

You stop being a practice that does buildings and become a practice that does two or three building types extremely well — mid-rise residential, schools, labs, logistics, senior living, whichever your portfolio and reputation actually support. Then you build a genuine kit of parts behind them: reusable assemblies, detail libraries validated against your jurisdictions' codes, standard specification sets, parametric massing and yield models, and an automated compliance-checking layer.

The economics change because your marginal cost per project collapses while your price does not have to. The second school costs you sixty percent of the first; the tenth costs thirty. That gap is your margin, and it belongs to you rather than to the client, because you are selling a solved problem rather than an hourly effort. It also gives you a defensible reason to be hired that is not price — you have done this exact thing forty times and can show the client a de-risked schedule.

The cost is that you have to kill generalism, publicly and irreversibly. You will turn down work. Some partners will hate this. The market risk also concentrates: if your chosen typology enters a downturn, you have no hedge. This is the most natural adjacency for a firm your size and the one AI most directly rewards, because AI is exceptionally good at applying a known pattern and mediocre at inventing one.

### 2. The upstream advisor — feasibility, entitlement and portfolio capacity

You move to the moment before there is a project. Site selection and acquisition support, yield and massing studies, planning and entitlement risk assessment, zoning envelope analysis, campus and estate capacity planning, portfolio rationalisation.

This is the highest-value decision point in the entire development chain and it is decided with the least information. A developer choosing between four sites is making a hundred-million-dollar decision on a two-week study you currently sell for a modest fixed fee, or give away to win the main commission. Generative massing and automated code-envelope analysis make it possible to test hundreds of scenarios in the time you currently test three, which turns a thin study into a genuinely rigorous piece of advice — and advice is priced on the value of the decision, not the hours behind it.

Headcount required is small and senior. There is no documentation production, so AI creates pure upside rather than deflation. Institutional clients are particularly well suited: universities, health systems, school authorities and municipalities all hold estates they must continuously plan, and that planning is a recurring need rather than a project event.

The risk is that this work is intermittent, relationship-dependent, and structurally hard to scale — it is a ten-person business inside a sixty-person one. It should be a spearhead, not the whole animal.

### 3. The institutional estate partner on long-term frameworks

Instead of bidding project by project, you convert your institutional half into multi-year term agreements — call-off frameworks with a university, a health system, a school district, a government department, a corporate occupier with a real estate portfolio.

The rates are lower. Everything else is better. Business development cost per project falls close to zero, revenue becomes forecastable, standardisation becomes possible because you are repeatedly serving one client's standards, and you accumulate client-specific knowledge that makes you progressively harder to replace. It also provides counter-cyclical ballast against a volatile commercial book, which matters enormously if you specialise elsewhere.

The catch is that public procurement is slow, often price-weighted, and you may need scale or a joint venture to qualify for the largest frameworks. But this is the most reliable way for a firm your size to build a floor under itself while it changes everything above the floor.

## What I'd actually recommend

A barbell, with a deliberate exit.

**At the high end**, build a small upstream advisory group — six to ten senior people, the sharpest strategic thinkers you have, sold as decision support rather than design. This sets the agenda, gets you into conversations early, generates downstream work on a negotiated rather than bid basis, and is immune to production deflation.

**At the base**, convert your institutional work toward long-term frameworks and estate partnerships. Lower rate, higher predictability, near-zero acquisition cost. This is your floor and it funds the transition.

**In the middle**, productise. Pick two typologies from your existing strength and build the reusable delivery system behind them. This is the machine, and it is the part AI makes profitable rather than the part AI destroys.

**And exit** generic mid-market commercial work where you are the fourth name on a bid list competing on fee. That work is where the compression is worst, where you have no defensible advantage, and where every hour you spend is an hour not spent building the other three. This will feel like voluntarily shrinking. It is, and that is the point — a smaller, differentiated revenue base is worth more than a larger commoditised one, and you cannot build the new model while feeding the old one.

## The part that usually kills this

Strategy documents like this fail at the organisational layer, not the market layer. Four things need explicit handling.

**The pyramid has to become a diamond, and you must not simply stop hiring juniors.** The shape you need is a small senior front end, a thick middle of experienced people who can direct and — critically — *verify* AI output, and a deliberately small junior intake. But if you stop hiring juniors entirely you have no seniors in a decade, and you will have solved a four-year problem by creating a twelve-year one. Hire fewer, and train them completely differently: on judgment, verification, and client interaction from month one, rather than on three years of drafting. The traditional apprenticeship worked because production taught judgment as a by-product. That link is broken and you have to rebuild it intentionally.

**You must stop measuring utilisation.** It is now actively misleading, because it rewards the behaviour that is killing you. Measure revenue per person, the proportion of revenue from negotiated versus competitively bid work, reuse rate across projects in your productised typologies, and win rate. Utilisation as a primary metric tells you how busy you are at doing the thing that no longer pays.

**Protect an investment budget and fund it by lowering utilisation, not raising it.** Five to eight percent of revenue, ring-fenced, with named people whose targets are explicitly reduced to build the delivery system and the advisory practice. If this work is expected to happen in evenings and gaps, it will not happen. That is the single most common failure mode.

**Deal with ownership economics honestly and early.** Partners whose equity value and personal identity are tied to the current model will resist, usually through delay rather than open opposition. Whoever runs a legacy commercial team that you intend to wind down needs to be told what their future looks like in the new structure before they work it out for themselves.

Rough sequencing: in the first ninety days, decide the typologies and stop bidding the worst of the commercial work. Within twelve months, stand up the advisory group and ship the first version of the delivery system on live projects. Within twenty-four months, have at least one framework agreement signed and be able to demonstrate margin improvement on repeat typology work. By year four, the majority of revenue should come from negotiated, framework, or productised work rather than open bids.

## What could go wrong

Worth naming the honest risks. AI may commoditise upstream advisory too — if generative massing becomes free and universal, feasibility studies deflate the same way documentation did, and your defence has to be judgment and client trust rather than the analysis itself. Outcome-based and equity-linked fees carry liability and insurance implications your current cover almost certainly does not contemplate. Institutional procurement can be every bit as price-driven as commercial. Specialisation concentrates market risk. And transitions of this kind cause talent flight, usually of exactly the mid-level people the new model depends on most.

None of these is a reason not to move. They are reasons to move deliberately rather than all at once.
