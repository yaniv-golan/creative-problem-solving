## Assumptions

You gave me the situation but not the numbers, so I've assumed the following. If any of these are materially wrong, the conclusions bend but the framework doesn't.

Revenue is somewhere around $11–13M, based on 60 staff at typical mid-market revenue per head. The firm is partner-owned with no outside capital and no meaningful balance sheet beyond working capital. Net margin is currently thin — high single digits at best, possibly lower after the utilisation push. You own no proprietary IP, no configurator, no product, no software. Your commercial work is won substantially on competitive fee, and your institutional work runs through frameworks, panels or repeat-owner relationships. "Institutional" means health, education, civic or government estate. And the fee compression you're seeing is 10–20% against comparable work three years ago.

---

## The diagnosis

The two levers you've already pulled — utilisation and cheaper juniors — are both cost-side moves inside an unchanged business model. That's the tell. It means the model itself is what's failing, and you've been optimising a machine that is producing something the market has decided to pay less for.

Here is the mechanism, stated plainly. You sell the production of a documentation set, priced against hours or against a percentage of construction cost. AI is collapsing the cost of producing that documentation set. In a market where you win on competitive fee, productivity gains do not become margin. They become the new price. Your competitors will get the same tools, bid the savings away, and the client will capture 100% of the benefit. You will run faster and end up in the same place, or slightly behind, because you'll have paid for the tooling.

This is not a prediction. It is what happens to every service that gets an order-of-magnitude productivity shock without a corresponding shift in what's being sold. The fee compression you're feeling now is the leading edge, not the event.

The second-order damage is worse and less visible. Cheaper juniors plus AI means you are hollowing out the layer that used to become your future associates and principals. Historically your juniors learned judgement by grinding through detail. If AI does the grinding and you've hired for cheapness rather than trajectory, you're not training anyone. In eight years you will have partners and no bench. That decision needs reversing regardless of which strategy you pick.

So: four more years is roughly the right horizon for your instinct. My read is that the current model doesn't break in four years — it degrades continuously, margin thins toward zero, and somewhere in year three or four you take a merger offer from a larger platform at a low multiple because you have no choice. That is the default path. Everything below is an argument with that default.

## The only four places margin survives

If AI-driven speed gets competed away, the question becomes: what *can't* be competed away? There are exactly four answers I can defend, and every strategic option in this memo is a combination of them.

**Reuse you own.** If you've solved a problem once and can redeploy the solution at near-zero marginal cost, and the client can't get that solution elsewhere, you keep the gain. This is productisation: kits of parts, configurators, validated detail libraries, tested typology models. The margin lives in the gap between your cost to redeploy and the market's cost to originate.

**Risk you absorb.** Clients pay premiums to move uncertainty off their books. Fixed price with a real guarantee, programme certainty, approval certainty, cost-outturn certainty, or design-build delivery. If you carry risk that others won't, that's a priced position, not a commodity.

**Access you control.** If you hold the client's standards, their space data, their model estate, their compliance history — you become expensive to remove. Not through a contract lock but through accumulated context. Recurring revenue lives here.

**Scarcity you hold.** Deep regulatory, technical or sectoral expertise that takes years to build and can't be prompted into existence. Complex healthcare compliance, lab and containment, listed-building and heritage intervention, fire strategy post-regulatory-reform, embodied carbon and retrofit performance. Non-discretionary, technically dense, and demand is rising.

Note what is not on that list: design quality. At 60 people in a mixed commercial and institutional market, design excellence is a qualifier that gets you on the shortlist. It is almost never the thing that lets you hold price. I say this knowing it is the least welcome sentence in this document, and I'd rather you argue with it now than discover it in year three.

## Six things you could be

I've deliberately kept these distinct rather than blending them, so the partnership can argue about real alternatives.

### 1. The typology factory

Pick two or three typologies you already do repeatedly — outpatient and diagnostic facilities, K-12 additions and modernisations, mid-rise multifamily, distribution and light industrial — and stop treating each as a fresh project. Build a genuine product: a parametric kit of parts, a validated detail set, pre-solved code and accessibility strategies, embedded cost models, and a configurator that lets you produce a credible scheme in days rather than weeks.

You then stop selling hours and start selling per bed, per key, per classroom, per square foot, at a fixed price with a guaranteed programme. Your cost per project falls by 40–60% after the second or third deployment. The client sees speed and certainty. You keep the difference, because your competitor is starting from a blank file.

This is the highest-margin play available to you and the only one that turns AI from a threat into an asset you own. It is also capital-intensive by professional-services standards: expect 12–18 months and something like $600K–1M of unbilled investment to get the first typology genuinely productised, and it requires a full-time product owner with authority to refuse bespoke variation. Firms fail at this almost exclusively through bespoke creep — every project gets "just one" exception, the product decays into a template folder, and the margin never materialises.

### 2. The institutional estate partner

Stop selling projects to institutions and start selling standing capability. Target six to ten owners — a health system, two or three universities, a school district, a municipality, a government estate — and contract as their embedded architectural function on multi-year retainer or framework. You hold their design standards, their space data, their model estate, their compliance record, their long-range capital plan. Projects flow from that position rather than being competed for individually.

The economics change shape entirely. Revenue becomes recurring and forecastable instead of lumpy. Business development cost collapses. You stop bidding. Your margin comes from *access you control* and from the compounding advantage of knowing an estate better than anyone else can afford to learn it.

This is, on my read, the strongest single move for a practice with your institutional weight. It's also the slowest — 18–30 months to land the first two or three anchor relationships — and it carries a real trap: retainers can become low-margin captivity, where you're the cheap in-house team doing unglamorous work with no leverage. The defence is to price on outcome and capability, never on headcount, and to pair it with option 1 so that the delivery work running through the retainer is productised rather than bespoke.

### 3. Upstream: the pre-design and portfolio advisor

The highest-value decisions in the built environment happen before there is a project — site capacity, portfolio rationalisation, entitlement risk, feasibility, disposal and repurposing analysis. This work is barely fee-compressed because it's priced against the decision's consequence, not against drawing production. And AI makes it dramatically better: you can now generate and test hundreds of massing, zoning and financial-envelope options in the time it used to take to draw three.

Revenue per head here is 2–3x your current delivery average. Fixed advisory fees, retainers, and in some cases success-linked structures.

The catch, and it's a serious one for you: this business needs eight excellent people, not sixty. It's a superb front door — it feeds pipeline into options 1 and 2 and it repositions you in the client's mind from vendor to advisor — but it cannot on its own absorb your overhead. Treat it as the tip of the spear, not the body.

### 4. Downstream: risk-bearing delivery

Move toward design-build, developer-side delivery, or absorbing technical scope that contractors currently price into their margin. You capture money where the money actually is, and you get paid for *risk you absorb*.

The upside is real and the market rewards it. But be honest about what it requires: a balance sheet you don't have, professional indemnity and surety arrangements you don't currently carry, and a genuinely different institutional temperament. A partnership that has spent thirty years managing risk away does not become a risk-taking organisation by resolution. If you go here, go via a partial version — carry programme and cost guarantees on productised typologies first, where your data actually supports the guarantee, and see whether the firm has the stomach for it before committing capital.

### 5. Regulatory and performance specialism

Regulation is expanding faster than design fees are compressing, and compliance spend is non-discretionary. Fire strategy, accessibility, embodied carbon, energy performance, resilience, and above all the decarbonisation and retrofit of institutional estates — which is a two-decade structural wave with mandated funding behind it, not a market cycle.

This is *scarcity you hold*. It is defensible, it compounds, and it pairs naturally with option 2 because institutional owners have vast retrofit obligations and no internal capacity to plan them. It's also the most straightforward for you to start: it's an acquisition-and-hire problem plus a positioning problem, not a business-model rebuild.

### 6. Become a node in a platform

Sell, or merge into, a larger multidisciplinary or PE-backed platform, and run your practice as a sector or geographic unit within it. This is a legitimate answer to "what else could we be," and I include it because consolidation is the dominant force in your size bracket right now and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

The critical point is that the multiple you get is a function of what you've already built. A 60-person firm selling hours at 6% net margin is worth very little and has no negotiating position. A firm with two productised typologies, four anchor framework relationships and 40% recurring revenue is worth several times more and can dictate terms. **So even if consolidation is your intended destination, the work below is what you should do first.** The strategies are not alternatives to selling; they are what makes selling worth doing.

## The thing nobody wants to say about being 60 people

Sixty is close to the worst size in this market. You're too large to command boutique scarcity pricing and carry a boutique's low overhead, and far too small to win on platform scale, geographic reach or balance sheet. You have a mid-size firm's cost structure and a small firm's pricing power. Most of your fee compression is a symptom of occupying that position rather than of anything you've done wrong.

There are three ways out and only three.

Shrink deliberately to 25–30 people doing advisory and specialist work at 2–3x revenue per head — a smaller, far more profitable, far more resilient firm. This is emotionally the hardest and financially the safest.

Grow past 150 through merger or acquisition, buying the scale and specialisms you can't build fast enough. This requires appetite for integration risk and probably outside capital.

Or restructure around a permanent core of roughly 25 senior people plus productised systems and elastic contract capacity, so that headcount flexes with demand instead of sitting as fixed cost through every downturn.

What does not work is staying at 60 doing the same thing more efficiently. That is the position you're currently in, and it is the one the market is actively pricing out.

## What I would actually do

If this were my partnership, I'd run three of the six in a deliberate configuration rather than picking one.

**Spine: the institutional estate partner (option 2).** It fits the work you already have, it converts lumpy project revenue into recurring revenue, and it's the hardest position for a competitor to attack because it's built on accumulated context rather than price.

**Engine: the typology factory (option 1), aimed at two typologies inside that institutional estate.** This is what stops the retainer relationship from becoming low-margin captivity. The framework gives you guaranteed repeat volume, which is precisely the condition under which productisation pays back.

**Front door: pre-design advisory (option 3), staffed thin and senior.** Six to eight people, no more. It repositions the firm, it originates the framework conversations, and it carries the highest margin per head in the business.

**Bolt on regulatory and retrofit depth (option 5) through hiring and possibly a small acquisition,** because it's the fastest-moving demand pool in institutional estate and it makes the spine stickier.

**Defer risk-bearing delivery (option 4)** until you have two years of productised cost data, at which point your guarantees are underwritten by evidence rather than optimism.

And exit — genuinely exit, not de-prioritise — open competitive fee bidding for generic commercial work where you have no reuse advantage. That work is where the compression is worst and where you have none of the four margin sources. It feels like it's covering overhead. It is consuming the senior attention required to build everything above.

## Sequencing over four years

**Year one — evidence and commitment.** Run true project-level profitability across the last three years; most firms discover 20–30% of their work is loss-making and cross-subsidised, and you cannot make good choices without this. Select two typologies on volume and repeatability. Appoint a product owner with real authority. Open serious conversations with four to six target institutional anchors. Stop bidding generic commercial. Reverse the junior hiring policy — hire fewer, better, and redefine the role around verifying and directing AI output rather than producing it.

**Year two — build and land.** Ship typology one and deploy it on live work. Sign the first two anchor framework agreements. Stand up the advisory group with senior hires. Begin the pyramid-to-diamond restructure: fewer production staff, materially more mid-level people capable of judging machine output, more sector and regulatory depth. Expect this to be the year the partnership's resolve is tested, because the investment is visible and the return isn't yet.

**Year three — price differently.** Move productised work to fixed unit pricing with programme guarantees. Ship typology two. Add two more anchors. Target 25% of revenue recurring. Revisit partner compensation and ownership, which is almost certainly still structured to reward billable hours and personal client control — the two behaviours you now need least.

**Year four — mix.** Target roughly 40% recurring framework revenue, 30% productised fixed-price at double your current delivery margin, 30% bespoke and specialist. Net margin in the high teens. At that point you are a genuinely different business, and you also have — should you want it — a real negotiating position with any acquirer.

## How this fails

Bespoke creep kills the product. Every exception is individually reasonable and collectively fatal.

The partnership hedges. It approves the strategy, funds it at 40%, and keeps chasing the commercial bids because they're familiar and they cover this quarter. This is the most likely failure mode by a wide margin, and it is worse than doing nothing, because you pay the investment cost and get none of the position.

Identity resistance. Some of your partners became architects to design buildings, not to run a productised delivery business or an advisory practice. That's a legitimate position and it deserves an honest conversation rather than being papered over — including, possibly, a conversation about who leads what, or who leaves.

The retainer trap. Frameworks won on price rather than capability become high-volume, low-margin captivity that's hard to escape and consumes your best people.

And the timing risk: this is a three-to-four year rebuild, funded from a thin current margin, and there will very likely be a downturn inside that window. Which is an argument for starting now, while you still have the choice, rather than in year two when you don't.

---

## The one-line version

You cannot keep AI's productivity gains by selling the same thing faster — the market takes them. You keep them by owning reuse, absorbing risk, controlling access, or holding scarce expertise. Everything else is running harder inside a model that is being priced out from under you.
