Eighteen months is a suspiciously specific number, and that's the most useful thing you've told me. People don't drift out at a consistent interval — a consistent interval means there's a mechanism. Two candidates are worth ruling out before anything else.

The first is equity shape rather than comp level. A one-year cliff plus a few months of watching the rest vest slowly, with no refresh grant until year three, produces exactly this pattern. You can benchmark and match total comp at offer time and still have a package whose retentive pull collapses around month 18 while a competitor's fresh four-year grant looks enormous by comparison. "We match" is a statement about the level; departures cluster around the slope. Worth pulling your grant schedules and refresh policy and seeing whether the cliff lines up with the exits.

The second is that 18 months is roughly how long it takes a senior engineer to fully absorb your system, ship the thing they were hired to ship, and then see their next two years clearly — and find that it looks like the last six months. Senior people rarely leave over the work being hard; they leave over it being finished, or over the ratio of interesting problems to maintenance toil quietly flipping. The questions that matter there are whether your seniors own problems or own tickets, whether there's anything legible above senior (a staff level with real definition and actual occupants, not an aspirational box on a ladder doc), and whether they're in the room where technical decisions get made or receiving those decisions.

On diagnosis: exit interviews will not tell you this. People leaving are polite, want a reference, and reach for the answer that burns nothing down. The better instrument is talking to the ones who stayed past 18 months and asking what made them stay, and running stay conversations at month nine or ten — before the decision is made, when it's still a describable frustration rather than a rationalization. Also check whether the exits cluster by manager or team rather than by tenure. If three of your last five came from the same org, you have a manager problem wearing a retention problem's clothes.

The thing I'd resist is the counter-offer reflex. Retention bought at the resignation stage has a short half-life and it teaches everyone the price of asking.
